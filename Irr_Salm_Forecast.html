Methodology

The Ecological Cascade

Salmonellosis outbreaks in humans and wildlife are connected to climate through a chain of ecological processes that unfold over the course of the year.

🌡️
Climate Anomalies
Spring & summer temperature and precipitation signals
🌲
Conifer Seed Crops
Masting years drive food availability for irruptive finches
🐦
Irruptive Finches
Poor seed crops push birds south in large, dense flocks
🦠
Salmonella Outbreaks
Outbreaks in birds can spill over to humans and domestic animals

Full methodology is described in: Tonelli et al. (2026). An ecological cascade links climatic variability to avian irruptions and zoonotic salmonellosis.

Public Guidance

What To Do

Knowing what to look for — and how to respond — can protect your household, pets, and local birds during an outbreak.

Signs of Salmonella infection in birds

Ruffled/puffed-up feathers Lethargic, easy to approach Unwilling to move Trouble swallowing Diarrhea

If you see sick or dead birds

  • Remove feeders & bird baths for at least 2 weeks
  • Keep pets away from sick birds and feeder areas
  • Contact your local wildlife health agency
  • Dispose of dead birds using a gloved hand or plastic bag
  • Wash hands with soap and water after any contact

Preventive feeder hygiene

  • Clean feeders with a 10% bleach solution monthly
  • Rake up hulls and droppings below feeders regularly
  • Use tube feeders — they reduce fecal contamination
  • Avoid platform feeders during known outbreak years
  • Do not handle birds with bare hands

Human & pet health

  • Salmonella can infect cats, dogs, and rarely humans
  • Symptoms: fever, vomiting, diarrhea — seek care if severe
  • Immunocompromised individuals face higher risk
  • Do not let children handle sick or dead birds
  • Report unusual pet illness to your vet
